Where the stroke folds

The central segment is the only part of the stroke the clock doesn't already fix. Fifteen constructions for it — and one piece of geometry that rules out the obvious half of them. Centrelines only; no width envelope.

01What ships today

src/c/main.c — calculate_pivot_point() and build_centerline().

The connector's endpoints are already fixed by the time: A, the inner end of the hour stem at 0.45·R, and B, the inner end of the minute stem at 0.54·R. Its tangent at A is the inward hour radial, its tangent at B the outward minute radial. Everything between them is decided by a single guide point, and the minimum-bending solver then picks three derivative vectors around it. The guide point is placed here:

d = 0.15·R · s(|sin Δ|) · √cos(Δ/2)
direction = normalise(ûh + ûm)  // outward bisector
where s(x) = 1.5x / (1 + 0.5x), Δ = angle between the hands

It works, and at both ends of the range it lands where it should. But nothing in it is derived from the drawing, and that shows up as seven separate problems.

  1. Two shaping factors doing one job. |sin Δ| and √cos(Δ/2) are both bumps that vanish at Δ = 0° and 180°. Multiplied, they produce a peak of 13.1 px at Δ ≈ 74° that nobody chose — it's the product of two arbitrary curves.
  2. |sin Δ| isn't monotone in the separation. Δ = 30° and Δ = 150° get the same pull, even though one is a near-reversal and the other is nearly straight. The turn the tangent has to make — not its sine — is the quantity the shape actually depends on.
  3. 0.15·R is a free constant. It isn't tied to a stem length, a hand length, or a stroke width. 13.1 px of drift is more than a fifth of the hour hand, and the fold sits more than 10 px off the pin for 324 of the 720 minutes in a twelve-hour cycle — not an edge case, but 45% of the day.
  4. The pivot only reaches the pin by coincidence. Both factors happen to vanish at overlap and at opposition. That's the "inelegant forcing" — the right answer arrives at the two endpoints for arithmetic reasons rather than geometric ones, and nothing constrains what happens in between.
  5. The guide point sits outside the pin. This is the structural fault, and section 02 is about it.
  6. The pivot's arc-length position is quantised. guide_index is rounded to a whole sample and then clamped into [first+2, last−2], so the normalised pivot position that drives the width profile jumps by up to a full segment as the hands move.
  7. The energy isn't intrinsic. The bending form is minimised parametrically with the parameter lengths set to |AG| and |GB|, so moving the guide point changes the parameterisation as well as the shape. Combined with the pixel-valued MAX_SOLVER_RESULT_MAGNITUDE 100.0f guard and the non-negative clamp that can silently zero a radial tangent, it is a good smoother rather than a definition of the curve.

02The connector is a corner, and its apex is the pin

One observation that collapses the whole design space.

The tangent at A points straight at the watch centre. The tangent at B points straight away from it. Both tangent lines therefore pass through the pin — so the connector is not a free-form join at all. It is a corner-rounding problem whose apex is the true centre, whose legs are 0.45·R and 0.54·R long, and whose apex angle is exactly Δ, the angle between the hands. The tangent has to turn by

Θ = 180° − Δ

and that number is handed to you by the clock. So the only real freedom left is how that fixed turn is distributed along the connector. Nothing else. Two consequences follow, and between them they decide the study.

(a) Reaching the pin needs concentrated turning

A curve that passes exactly through the apex is locally straight there, so it enters and leaves along opposite directions. If Δ ≠ 180°, the rest of the turn has to happen somewhere — and the closer you want the fold to the pin, the more of it must happen in the sliver of arc nearest the pin. In the limit, the fold is a corner. Conversely, round a 180° reversal with any bounded curvature and the tangent points run off to infinity; with them pinned at 0.45·R and 0.54·R, the curve has to bulge instead. That bulge is the hairpin.

This is not a matter of taste. Rows X1 and X2 below are the two textbook "most natural curve" answers — an equal-tangent circular biarc and a G² Euler spiral solved for closure — and at overlap they park the fold 45 px and 43 px from the pin, with the stroke visibly detached from the centre of the watch. A smooth-curvature connector cannot pivot at the centre. The kink isn't a compromise you make; it's the only way through.

(b) The pin has to be enclosed, not cut

A Bézier or conic lies inside the convex hull of its control points. If the hull has the pin as a vertex — true of the shipping guide point (A, G, B with the pin outside), of the corner conic (A, pin, B), and of the λ = 1 cubic — then the curve can only touch the pin by collapsing onto the corner itself. Pivot accuracy and roundness become strictly traded: B1 sits 17 px off at 82°; B1p buys back the overlap case and pays 19 px at 110°. Families whose hull contains the pin — A1, B2o, every swing row — break the trade: the fold can sit on the pin and be rounded.

07Where I'd take it

Ranked by what they buy against what they cost.

1

Return the centre from calculate_pivot_point()row A1

The smallest possible change, and it removes the entire problem. The min-bending solver, the Hermite sampling, the arc-length cache and the width code all stay exactly as they are; the guide point simply stops moving. Because the solver leaves the guide derivative free, the hull encloses the pin and the corner still rounds — it just rounds around the pin instead of away from it.

static Vec2 calculate_pivot_point(Vec2 center, ...) {
  return center;
}
// MAX_PIVOT_OFFSET_RATIO and PIVOT_PULL_BIAS both go away

Two magic constants deleted, none added; ~60 lines of trigonometry deleted. The fold lands on the pin at every minute of the day, and the silhouette stays recognisably the one you have. If the corner reads too hard once the width envelope is back on, pair it with row A1s — equalising the two stem radii at 0.50·R makes the corner isoceles, which is one fewer asymmetry to reason about in the width code.

Ship this first
2

Reformulate the stroke as a swing about the pinrow CΔ

The stronger answer, if you're willing to replace the geometry rather than patch it. Drop the stem/connector split entirely and write the whole centreline as one point on a rotating ray with a signed radius:

u : +0.60·R → −0.90·R  // signed radius, hour tip to minute tip
ψ(u) = θh + Θ · smoothstep((h − u) / 2h)
P(u) = u · û(ψ(u))
with h = 0.40·R · Δ/180°

Everything falls out of that. For |u| > h the angle is constant, so the stems are exactly straight radial segments — no separate construction, no G¹ join to get wrong. u = 0 is the pin, so the fold is on the centre by definition rather than by arithmetic. The fold's interior angle is exactly Δ, which means the stroke literally shows you the angle between the hands. The curvature at the fold is 1.5·|Θ|/h, so h reads as one physical quantity — how tight the pen turns — and scaling it with Δ gives a clean cusp at overlap opening into a rounded corner as the hands separate, with a dead-straight line at opposition.

It also uses the hand lengths directly (they are the two ends of u), which was the thing you asked for. And it is far cheaper: no 4×4 solve, no Newton square roots, no guide index, no fallback path — one sin/cos lookup per sample.

The design I'd actually pursue
3

Shrink the corner instead of moving the bellyrow E1

The one candidate that keeps a genuinely smooth, kink-free connector at every angle and still lands on the pin at overlap. Keep any smooth primitive you like — here it's the equal-tangent biarc from the control set — but make the corner region itself vanish as the hands converge: run both stems in to r = 0.50·R · Δ/180° rather than to fixed fractions. The hairpin doesn't disappear, it just shrinks below visibility along with everything else.

1.5 px at overlap, 10.4 px at 82°. Worth keeping in your pocket if, once the width envelope goes back on, the kink turns out to be the thing you don't want after all.

Fallback if you reject the kink
4

The loop, if you want onerow D1

The swing with one extra revolution folded into the turn window gives a small ink eye at the pin at every angle, ~9 px across. It reads well and it is genuinely calligraphic, but it's a constant feature rather than a response to the time, so it changes what the watch is. If you want the loop only where the geometry asks for it, keep the fixed-window swing (C1) instead: it produces an eye of diameter h within a couple of minutes of the hands crossing and a plain corner everywhere else.

Available, not recommended as the target
×

Ruled outrows B1, B1p, B2, X1, X2

The corner conics are elegant — apex exactly on the pin, weight w = tan(Θ/2), zero free constants, and the cut fraction 1/(1+w) comes out within 4% of the dead-simple Δ/180° — but 02(b) is fatal: they cut the corner, so they cannot hug the pin without degenerating into it. The λ = 1 cubic is the worst case in the study, floating 12.4 px off the pin at overlap where the requirement is tightest. Push its handles to λ = 4/3 (row B2o) and it becomes a perfectly good member of the enclosing family — but by then row A1 gets you the same result without touching the sampling code.

08What this study deliberately ignores

Only the centreline, as you asked. Three things will want a second look once the width envelope goes back on, and all three get easier under any of the enclosing-family recommendations:

Self-overlap ink. With the fold on the pin, the two branches meet at the centre at their narrowest point, so branch_clearance is now a clean function of Δ alone — 2 · rc · sin(Δ/2) with a single rc if you also symmetrise the stems.

The pivot's arc-length position. Under the swing there is no guide_index to quantise: the pivot is exactly u = 0, and its normalised arc-length position can be computed in closed form instead of looked up.

Miter blow-up at the cusp. A sharp fold with a normal-offset polygon will spike at the corner. The swing's h is the direct control for that: it sets the minimum radius of curvature, so it can be floored at something like half the stroke width rather than tuned by eye.
